# Afghanistan Climate-FCV Evidence Dossier

## Executive assessment

- Named provincial food-access constraints and July 2026 flood losses add local vulnerability detail, but the seasonal outlook is not a climate-model forecast. [AFG-E-106, AFG-E-107]
- Conflict-linked forest loss and weak capital/support are described adaptation constraints. Reported FAO-supported household gains are historical and do not establish national or causal effects. [AFG-E-102, AFG-E-105, AFG-E-108]

Critical remaining gaps:
- Climate-projection gap: The principal official detailed projection source remains the 2017 SNC using CORDEX/RCP-era modelling. A current official high-resolution CMIP6 assessment, especially for seasonal precipitation, extremes, snowpack, glacier mass, GLOFs and basin hydrology, was not located in the verified set. []
- Snow/glacier gap: Snowmelt and glacier/GLOF pressures are recognized in national policy, but current observational and projection evidence with basin-level locators is insufficient for strong screening classifications. []
- Causal Climate-FCV gap: No national study in the verified set identifies a causal effect of drought, heat, flood or water scarcity on violence after controlling adequately for conflict history, armed actors, land tenure, governance, markets and political mobilization. The strongest mechanism evidence is local and qualitative. []
- Geographic/population gap: Recent direct studies concentrate on Balkh, Herat, Kandahar, Kabul and Nangarhar. Pastoral/Kuchi communities, remote highlands, inaccessible districts, persons with disabilities, some minority groups and women without telephone access are underrepresented. []
- Evaluation gap: No fully independent, counterfactual impact evaluation of a post-2021 climate-adaptation or Climate-FCV programme was located. The two verified terminal evaluations are externally conducted but commissioned within the UNDP-GEF evaluation system and concern pre- or immediately post-transition projects. []
- Unintended-effect gap: There is little evaluated evidence on whether adaptation programmes create elite capture, unequal water access, land disputes, exclusion, maladaptation, maintenance burdens, gendered risks or other unintended effects. []
- Differentiated-vulnerability gap: Gender and return status are comparatively well documented, but rigorous intersections among gender, disability, ethnicity, pastoral identity, age, location, tenure and climate exposure are much thinner. []
- FCV-to-climate evidence gap: The reverse direction is credible - political restrictions, aid decline, institutional disruption and donor engagement affect adaptation capacity - but quantitative evidence on how much these conditions reduce resilience or increase climate losses is weak. []
- Institutional-capacity gap: Current evidence on de facto national ministries, provincial departments, districts, customary institutions, water-user institutions and community governance is fragmented. The 2015 NDC and 2017 SNC cannot be treated as descriptions of current institutional capacity. []
- Access/source gap: The original UN Women-hosted copy of AFG-SRC-013 was not reliably retrievable in this browsing session; the full report was verified through the public ecoi.net mirror, which also identifies and links the original UN Women source. Some very recent humanitarian products encountered during research did not yield fully verifiable PDF locators and were therefore excluded rather than retained. []
- Subnational climate-conflict pathways remain unevenly documented; the SIPRI synthesis supports several explicit mechanisms but does not isolate climate's contribution to conflict outcomes. []
- The full FAO policy brief was inaccessible through its DOI link; its publisher summary supports only the reported E-RIMA results and methodology. []
- The 2026 UN Results Report PDF could not be inspected because of its size, so it was excluded. []
- Current provincial-level evidence on adaptation barriers for women, people with disabilities and other groups was not expanded in this pass. []
- The SIPRI fact sheet is a secondary synthesis, including its 2025 migration estimate; no independent verification of the underlying mobility dataset was completed. []
- Provincial food-access forecasts and localized July 2026 flood losses are kept separate from causal climate-conflict claims. []
- Draft extension: human source review and approval remain pending. Lending eligibility is not an FCV classification. []

## Climate pressures and exposure

- Strong observed warming. [AFG-E-001]
- Flood impacts ≠ proven rainfall trend. [AFG-E-002]
- Mid-century warming across scenarios. [AFG-E-003]
- Precipitation change is region/season specific. [AFG-E-004]
- Recent synthesis confirms warming direction. [AFG-E-005]
- 2025 drought had broad livelihood exposure. [AFG-E-008]
- Drought dominated 2025 reported environmental exposure. [AFG-E-010]
- SIPRI/NUPI's April 2026 synthesis links conflict-driven deforestation with greater flash-flood exposure in broad eastern, northern and western zones. [AFG-E-102]
- FEWS NET reports late-July 2026 flooding damaged homes, infrastructure, cropland and standing crops in localized parts of Nangarhar, Paktika and Kabul; it expects household losses but no area-level food-security classification change. [AFG-E-107]

## Differentiated vulnerability

- Acute food insecurity remained widespread. [AFG-E-007]
- Food insecurity projected to remain high in 2026. [AFG-E-009]
- Drinking-water sufficiency worsened. [AFG-E-011]
- Recent returnees showed higher multidimensional deprivation. [AFG-E-012]
- Returns overlap with severe local water stress. [AFG-E-015]
- Mass returns are an independent vulnerability and service-pressure driver. [AFG-E-030]
- SIPRI/NUPI's April 2026 synthesis describes agricultural drought, low coping capacity and socioeconomic fragility across broad central, northern and northwestern zones; it does not provide provincial estimates. [AFG-E-101]
- FEWS NET expects consecutive drought years and below-average production to keep food and income access constrained in Daykundi, Faryab, Ghor, Nimroz, Badakhshan and Kunar, despite a near-average 2026 harvest elsewhere. [AFG-E-106]

## Coping and adaptive capacity

- Home-based coping can carry human-capital costs. [AFG-E-013]
- Women returnees face strong livelihood-capacity constraints. [AFG-E-014]
- Coping often depletes future resilience. [AFG-E-020]
- Gender restrictions amplify climate vulnerability. [AFG-E-022]
- The April 2026 SIPRI/NUPI synthesis describes displacement as a last resort where weak physical, financial and social capital and limited public support restrict adaptation. [AFG-E-105]

## Climate-FCV pathways

- Drought can deepen livelihood stress through agriculture, water, markets and aid constraints, potentially increasing harmful coping or grievances, but available evidence does not demonstrate that it causes violence. [AFG-P-001; AFG-E-003, AFG-E-004, AFG-E-007, AFG-E-008, AFG-E-020, AFG-E-021, AFG-E-025]
  - Documented impact: Crop/rangeland and water stress; reduced agricultural livelihood security.
  - FCV mediator: Income loss, food prices, debt, thin labour markets and reduced humanitarian assistance.
  - Possible consequence: Where household reserves, markets and assistance are weak, climate-related livelihood losses may increase negative coping, distress mobility and localized grievances; violence is not an automatic result.

- In surveyed Herat and Kandahar communities, drought and recharge constraints interact with returns, extraction and weak infrastructure; this compound water stress has been associated with localized tensions but not demonstrated organized conflict. [AFG-P-002; AFG-E-011, AFG-E-015, AFG-E-016, AFG-E-030]
  - Documented impact: Difficult household water access and declining groundwater availability.
  - FCV mediator: Large return flows, population concentration, over-extraction, weak infrastructure, water costs and queues at shared sources.
  - Possible consequence: Local water disputes or host-returnee tension may increase where scarce supplies and costs cannot be managed equitably.

  - Alternative explanations: ['Groundwater over-pumping', 'urban growth', 'water infrastructure', 'pricing', 'salinity and governance']
  - Uncertainty: The relative contribution of drought versus return flows and long-term over-extraction is not quantified.
- Balkh evidence documents a gradual drought-livelihood-displacement process, but household resources and social/service conditions substantially mediate whether movement occurs. [AFG-P-003; AFG-E-017, AFG-E-019, AFG-E-020]
  - Documented impact: Loss of livelihoods/assets and deteriorating access to food, water and shelter.
  - FCV mediator: Household savings/assets, debt, rental costs, service access and social networks.
  - Possible consequence: When coping options are exhausted, households may relocate; displacement can then reproduce exposure and increase protection and livelihood risks.

  - Alternative explanations: ['Earlier conflict displacement', 'rent', 'employment', 'family structure', 'discrimination and pre-existing poverty']
  - Uncertainty: Purposive qualitative data cannot estimate how frequently drought produces displacement nationally.
- Political and social restrictions can narrow women’s options for responding to climate shocks, although household resources and provincial context produce substantial variation. [AFG-P-004; AFG-E-003, AFG-E-008, AFG-E-011, AFG-E-013, AFG-E-014, AFG-E-022, AFG-E-025]
  - Documented impact: Greater need for income diversification, disaster assistance and health/WASH services.
  - FCV mediator: Restrictions on women’s work, mobility and public participation, plus sociocultural norms and shortages of women service providers.
  - Possible consequence: Women and female-headed households may have fewer adaptation and recovery options and may depend more heavily on home-based or informal coping.
  - Link evidence: {'pressure': ['AFG-E-003', 'AFG-E-008'], 'impact': ['AFG-E-011', 'AFG-E-014'], 'mediator': ['AFG-E-014', 'AFG-E-022'], 'consequence': ['AFG-E-013', 'AFG-E-022']}
  - Alternative explanations: ['Poverty', 'caregiving responsibilities', 'education', 'location', 'labour demand and household composition']
  - Uncertainty: Restrictions and enforcement vary spatially; available studies do not estimate an independent climate-adaptation effect.
- Aid and institutional constraints plausibly reduce climate-recovery capacity by weakening already strained services, but climate-specific outcome effects remain poorly evaluated. [AFG-P-005; AFG-E-007, AFG-E-008, AFG-E-011, AFG-E-025, AFG-E-026, AFG-E-030]
  - Documented impact: High food/water needs and increased demand for recovery support.
  - FCV mediator: Declining international assistance, political constraints on engagement and non-standard delivery channels.
  - Possible consequence: Lower service and adaptation coverage may prolong vulnerability after climate shocks and weaken household buffers.

  - Alternative explanations: ['Programme prioritization', 'logistics', 'geographic access', 'workforce shortages and general fiscal constraints']
  - Uncertainty: No study located quantitatively estimates how much aid contraction changed climate-specific recovery outcomes.
- Climate stress can intensify the importance of shared water, but allocation and political decisions are indispensable mediators; water cooperation can also support dialogue. [AFG-P-006; AFG-E-024]
  - Documented impact: Greater pressure on available water.
  - FCV mediator: Dam/irrigation decisions, allocation rules, weak transboundary mechanisms and bilateral politics.
  - Possible consequence: Water disputes may contribute to interstate tension; conversely, cooperative water governance may create dialogue opportunities.

  - Documented impact: Continuing need for climate information, DRR, resilient livelihoods and water management.
  - FCV mediator: Political transition in 2021, altered institutional structures, donor engagement constraints and project suspension.
  - Possible consequence: Institutional learning, national/provincial capacity building and continuity of adaptation programmes may weaken even when some community assets remain.

- Informal solidarity is a documented coping asset in some communities, but its peace and long-term resilience effects have not been independently evaluated. [AFG-P-008; AFG-E-015, AFG-E-017, AFG-E-019, AFG-E-020]
  - Documented impact: High unmet needs among displaced and returning households.
  - FCV mediator: Strength of family, neighbourhood, charity and community-support networks.
  - Possible consequence: Strong informal support may reduce immediate exclusion, destitution and host-returnee tension, although its durability under prolonged poverty is uncertain.

  - Uncertainty: No evaluation measures whether solidarity mechanisms actually prevent conflict or improve long-run resilience.

## Resilience and peace-supporting capacities

- Surveyed water stress and tensions had multiple interacting drivers. [AFG-E-016]
- Drought -> livelihood erosion -> displacement, locally documented. [AFG-E-017]
- Conflict evidence in Balkh is mixed, not deterministic. [AFG-E-018]
- Informal solidarity is a resilience resource. [AFG-E-019]
- Climate-FCV effects are mediated. [AFG-E-021]
- Shared water can generate both tension and cooperation. [AFG-E-024]
- SIPRI/NUPI's April 2026 synthesis describes a conditional mechanism: resource pressure can intensify land and water disputes, with de facto-authority involvement complicating farmer-herder relations. [AFG-E-103]
- SIPRI/NUPI reports 675,500 climate-related cross-border migrants in April-December 2025, particularly from Faryab, Ghor, Badghis and Balkh. [AFG-E-104]
