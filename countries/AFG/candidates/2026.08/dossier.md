# Afghanistan Climate-FCV Evidence Dossier

## Executive assessment

- Afghanistan faces substantial warming and recurrent drought/water stress, but precipitation and flood trends require careful regional and causal calibration. [AFG-E-001, AFG-E-002, AFG-E-003, AFG-E-004, AFG-E-005, AFG-E-008, AFG-E-010, AFG-E-011, AFG-P-001, AFG-P-002, AFG-P-003]
- The strongest Climate-FCV evidence concerns mediated effects through livelihoods, displacement, water access, services and institutional constraints - not a direct climate-to-conflict relationship. [AFG-E-015, AFG-E-016, AFG-E-017, AFG-E-018, AFG-E-021, AFG-E-022, AFG-E-023, AFG-E-024, AFG-E-025, AFG-E-030, AFG-P-001, AFG-P-002, AFG-P-003, AFG-P-004, AFG-P-005, AFG-P-006, AFG-P-007]
- Recovery and adaptation capacity is constrained by aid contraction, gender restrictions, institutional disruption and weak evidence systems, while community networks and externally monitored delivery provide some resilience. [AFG-E-006, AFG-E-013, AFG-E-014, AFG-E-019, AFG-E-022, AFG-E-023, AFG-E-024, AFG-E-025, AFG-E-026, AFG-E-027, AFG-E-028, AFG-E-029, AFG-P-004, AFG-P-005, AFG-P-007, AFG-P-008]

Critical remaining gaps:
- Climate-projection gap: The principal official detailed projection source remains the 2017 SNC using CORDEX/RCP-era modelling. A current official high-resolution CMIP6 assessment, especially for seasonal precipitation, extremes, snowpack, glacier mass, GLOFs and basin hydrology, was not located in the verified set. []
- Snow/glacier gap: Snowmelt and glacier/GLOF pressures are recognized in national policy, but current observational and projection evidence with basin-level locators is insufficient for strong screening classifications. []
- Causal Climate-FCV gap: No national study in the verified set identifies a causal effect of drought, heat, flood or water scarcity on violence after controlling adequately for conflict history, armed actors, land tenure, governance, markets and political mobilization. The strongest mechanism evidence is local and qualitative. []
- Geographic/population gap: Recent direct studies concentrate on Balkh, Herat, Kandahar, Kabul and Nangarhar. Pastoral/Kuchi communities, remote highlands, inaccessible districts, persons with disabilities, some minority groups and women without telephone access are underrepresented. []
- Evaluation gap: No fully independent, counterfactual impact evaluation of a post-2021 climate-adaptation or Climate-FCV programme was located. The two verified terminal evaluations are externally conducted but commissioned within the UNDP-GEF evaluation system and concern pre- or immediately post-transition projects. []
- Unintended-effect gap: There is little evaluated evidence on whether adaptation programmes create elite capture, unequal water access, land disputes, exclusion, maladaptation, maintenance burdens, gendered risks or other unintended effects. []
- Differentiated-vulnerability gap: Gender and return status are comparatively well documented, but rigorous intersections among gender, disability, ethnicity, pastoral identity, age, location, tenure and climate exposure are much thinner. []
- FCV-to-climate evidence gap: The reverse direction is credible - political restrictions, aid decline, institutional disruption and donor engagement affect adaptation capacity - but quantitative evidence on how much these conditions reduce resilience or increase climate losses is weak. []
- Institutional-capacity gap: Current evidence on de facto national ministries, provincial departments, districts, customary institutions, water-user institutions and community governance is fragmented. The 2015 NDC and 2017 SNC cannot be treated as descriptions of current institutional capacity. []
- Access/source gap: The original UN Women-hosted copy of AFG-SRC-013 was not reliably retrievable in this browsing session; the full report was verified through the public ecoi.net mirror, which also identifies and links the original UN Women source. Some very recent humanitarian products encountered during research did not yield fully verifiable PDF locators and were therefore excluded rather than retained. []

## Climate-FCV pathways

- Drought can deepen livelihood stress through agriculture, water, markets and aid constraints, potentially increasing harmful coping or grievances, but available evidence does not demonstrate that it causes violence. [AFG-P-001; AFG-E-003, AFG-E-004, AFG-E-007, AFG-E-008, AFG-E-020, AFG-E-021, AFG-E-025]
  - Documented impact: Crop/rangeland and water stress; reduced agricultural livelihood security.
  - FCV mediator: Income loss, food prices, debt, thin labour markets and reduced humanitarian assistance.
  - Possible consequence: Where household reserves, markets and assistance are weak, climate-related livelihood losses may increase negative coping, distress mobility and localized grievances; violence is not an automatic result.

## Geographic and livelihood-system differentiation

- National evidence is strongest for climate warming, IPC food insecurity, WoAA socioeconomic exposure and macro/service conditions. Regional contrasts are material: the SNC projects different precipitation responses across the North, Central Highlands, East, Hindu Kush and South, while WoAA found especially high reported drought exposure in the Central Highlands and sharp water-sufficiency deterioration in the Central Highlands and East. (UNFCCC1) []
- The direct Climate-FCV field evidence is considerably narrower. ACAPS evidence applies to Dehdadi, Nahr Shahi and Sholgareh in Balkh and must not be extrapolated to Afghanistan as a whole. Mercy Corps water findings apply to surveyed communities in Herat and Kandahar. UN Women/Samuel Hall findings apply to sampled women returnees linked to Herat, Nangarhar and Kabul. The 2019 adaptation evaluation applies to project locations in Panjshir, Balkh, Uruzgan and Herat, with particularly important security/access limitations for Uruzgan. (ACAPS12) []
- Conflict-control and humanitarian-access geography remains incompletely represented. Recent sources do not provide a systematic district-level comparison of adaptation delivery under different degrees of de facto authority control, local enforcement, remoteness or humanitarian access. High-mountain glacier/GLOF communities, many pastoral/Kuchi routes, isolated rural women, persons without telephone access, people with disabilities, and some ethnic-minority communities are particularly weakly represented. []
- Environmental and geological distinctions are important. The WoAA analysis explicitly distinguishes drought and flood from earthquakes, and this packet does not classify the 2025 earthquakes as climate-related. Likewise, groundwater depletion is treated as compound - drought/recharge interacts with abstraction, infrastructure, population and policy - rather than automatically labelled a climate impact. (UNDP2) []
- Evidence is strongest for agriculture, rural livelihoods, food security, drinking water/WASH, displacement/return, household poverty, humanitarian assistance and basic service delivery. Health is reasonably represented through service-access constraints, and household livelihood adaptation is covered through UNDP and UN Women evidence. []
- Coverage is thinner for energy and hydropower, glacier-fed basin hydrology, detailed pastoral systems, urban drainage/flood infrastructure, municipal finance, biodiversity/ecosystems, climate-sensitive disease attribution, transport-system resilience, and private-sector adaptation. Mining and environmental degradation appear in climate-security syntheses but should not be recoded as climate impacts without explicit linkage. []
