# Solomon Islands Climate-FCV Evidence Dossier

## Executive assessment

- Named Rendova communities and provincial water investments add local vulnerability and governance detail; project surveys and two-community fieldwork are not representative rankings. [SLB-E-101, SLB-E-102, SLB-E-105, SLB-E-106, SLB-E-108]
- A historical Honiara disaster-recovery grievance case illustrates a possible mechanism, but the same three-country study finds no significant aggregate disaster/temperature association with social conflict. [SLB-E-109, SLB-E-110, SLB-P-101]

Critical remaining gaps:
- No countrywide causal estimate of climate impacts on conflict or violence is established by these sources. []
- Full subnational coverage and independently evaluated long-term response effectiveness remain gaps. []
- Full underlying community datasets and current provincial conflict incidence were not inspected. []
- Draft collection pending human source review and approval. IDA eligibility is not an FCV classification. []
- National and selected local examples do not establish district-level coverage or causal attribution. []
- Selected-site relocation and land-access mechanism is conditional, without national causal attribution. []
- The evidence is site- and project-specific; it does not provide a comprehensive current vulnerability ranking across provinces or province-level conflict incidence. []
- The 2026 climate-conflict study reports one Honiara post-flood protest-turned-riot but finds no statistically significant aggregate disaster or temperature effect for its three-country panel; the event mechanism was not statistically tested. []
- No source located in the targeted searches showed insecurity or organized violence interrupting a named climate-adaptation delivery operation; no such reverse pathway is inferred from historical unrest. []

## Climate pressures and exposure

- Source dated 2020-10-26 reports: The 2015-16 El Niño drought affected Central, Rennell and Bellona, and Temotu; schools closed and communities prioritized water projects. [SLB-E-101]

## Differentiated vulnerability

- Karaina residents described coastal land loss; Tuwo households used preserved breadfruit as a buffer against drought and cyclone disruption. [SLB-E-001]
- Source dated 2020 reports: Baniata had 533 residents and eight-community Lokuru had 1,065; households relied on gardens, fisheries, forest nuts and groundwater for food and livelihoods. [SLB-E-105]
- Source dated 2020 reports: Both sites identified gardens, reefs and fish, water, rivers, shorelines and low-lying infrastructure as vulnerable; factors included hazard exposure, high dependence and weak resource management. [SLB-E-106]

## Coping and adaptive capacity

- Source dated 2020 reports: Workshops used local coordinators and women's and men's groups; priorities included leadership, health, farming, livelihood diversification, resource management and youth capacity. [SLB-E-107]

## Institutions and delivery systems

- UNDP identifies deficient legal frameworks, private interests and exclusion from land decisions as potential conflict drivers. [SLB-E-004]
- Source dated 2020-10-26 reports: Over 80 percent of CRISP subprojects involved water, mainly rainwater harvesting; dry-season shortages made it a priority in Malaita, Rennell and Central. [SLB-E-102]
- Source dated 2020-10-26 reports: Sixty-three of 65 communities completed their highest-ranked climate-risk investment with PMU support; two completed their next priority because of cost constraints. [SLB-E-103]
- Source dated 2020-10-26 reports: Tulagi's 260-metre shoreline works protected the road and foreshore trees during Tropical Cyclone Harold in April 2020. [SLB-E-104]
- Source dated 2020 reports: Participants said cultural change and weaker local governance reduced use of traditional tabu areas; they proposed a community marine protected area and fisheries rules. [SLB-E-108]
- Source dated 2026-08-25 reports: UNDP reports an expanded agroforestry nursery in West Kwarae, mangrove rehabilitation and a community centre in South Malaita, reforestation in West Are'are, and land restoration improving student access at Afio Primary School. [SLB-E-111]
- Source dated 2026-08-25 reports: UNDP reports that the Wahere Community Association built a seawall at Marau Sound, East Guadalcanal, to protect homes, infrastructure and livelihoods from coastal erosion and rising sea levels. [SLB-E-112]
- Source dated 2026-10-20 reports: The government reports that the NAP was under formulation in October 2026, drawing on a 12-sector CRVA with consultations in all nine provinces and SIIVA assessments in Isabel and Makira focused on daily climate and livelihood effects. [SLB-E-113]

## Climate-FCV pathways

- Climate pressures can interact with relocation land constraints and exclusion to create tensions; kinship networks and inclusive local decisions can support adaptation. [SLB-P-001; SLB-E-001, SLB-E-002, SLB-E-004, SLB-E-006]
  - Documented impact: Community research describes threatened land/livelihoods and movement away from exposed settlements.
  - FCV mediator: Limited relocation land, unequal access, customary tenure and community identity shape mobility options.
  - Possible consequence: Relocation can generate land disputes or social tensions when access and participation are not resolved.

  - Alternative explanations: ['Education, employment and family connections also drive mobility.', 'Land exclusion, external project design and existing governance disputes can cause tension independently of climate.']
  - Uncertainty: Conditional synthesis of selected-site research and contextual peacebuilding analysis, not a nationwide causal estimate. Source-specific examples do not establish that each mechanism occurs in every named site.
- One Honiara2014case links flood displacement, perceived inadequate rehabilitation and a protest-turned-riot; aggregate study findings do not establish a general disaster-conflict effect. [SLB-P-101; SLB-E-109, SLB-E-110]
  - Documented impact: People were displaced and faced disaster-related losses.
  - FCV mediator: Perceived inadequacy of government rehabilitation support generated recovery grievances.

## Resilience and peace-supporting capacities

- The research links environmental pressures with existing inequalities and mobility; limited relocation land can generate conflict even when kinship networks assist movers. [SLB-E-002]
- The findings recommend local leadership, traditional knowledge and inclusive planning for women, older people and persons with disabilities. [SLB-E-003]
- The programme proposes customary-tenure reforms and women's and youth participation in natural-resource decisions. [SLB-E-005]
- The paper cautions against direct climate-conflict links while identifying potential tensions around relocation, place-based identity and externally designed adaptation. [SLB-E-006]
- It recommends adaptation grounded in local worldviews and power relations, avoiding interventions that undermine community capacities. [SLB-E-007]
- Source dated 2026-01-22 reports: The authors describe an event in which people displaced by April 2014 floods to a Honiara informal settlement protested and rioted on 16 May, perceiving the government rehabilitation package as inadequate for their disaster losses. [SLB-E-109]
- Source dated 2026-01-22 reports: Across its Fiji, Solomon Islands and Vanuatu panel, the study found no statistically significant association between climate-related disasters or temperature extremes and social-conflict risk, including models separating protests and riots. [SLB-E-110]
- In October2026, officials stated that NAP design should respect customary land and include women, youth, older people and persons with disabilities; participation and equitable effects were not verified. [SLB-E-114]
