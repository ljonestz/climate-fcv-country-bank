- No primary source was identified in this search showing insecurity or violence obstructing climate adaptation, early warning or disaster response; this is a search gap, not evidence that such effects never occur. []
- The four-city vulnerability assessment is preliminary, relies on 2011 census indicators and did not quantitatively integrate adaptation capacity; its flood projections lack hydrological recalculation. []
- The IMF relocation estimate is national and does not identify the affected locations; SINAE provides named event locations but limited demographic, tenure or protection detail at site level. []

- IMF national economic and relocation findings must not be assigned equally to named study departments. Model shock responses are not calendar forecasts. []

## Climate pressures and exposure

- Source dated 2020-04-22 reports: Maps project hazards to 2044 and 2099; flood and drainage areas remain unchanged without hydrological modeling. Scenario: SSP/CMIP6; flood/drainage projections not hydrologically recalculated. [URY-E-106]
- Source dated 2024-01-08 reports: The October 2023–January 2024 Uruguay River flood affected Artigas, Cerro Largo, Paysandú, Salto and Tacuarembó; peak displacement was 3,511 people, over 90% self-evacuated, with the largest counts in Paysandú, Salto and Artigas. [URY-E-111]
- Source dated 2024-01-08 reports: In Paysandú, Diagonal San Félix, the waterfront, Unión Portuaria and the racetrack vicinity were among the hardest-hit areas; in Salto, the waterfront, Arenitas Blancas and Parque Harriague were most affected. [URY-E-112]
- Source dated 2025-01-10 reports: SINAE's 2024 table attributes 13,682 affected people and 5,090 affected homes to 40 floods; flood-row counts include 7,252 women, 6,430 men, 9,065 adults and 4,617 minors. Durazno, San José, Paysandú and Florida had the most people affected overall. [URY-E-114]
- Source dated 2025-01-10 reports: March rainfall totals doubled usual monthly averages; flooding reached Santa Lucía and 25 de Agosto in Canelones and Florida, places described as historically unaffected, and displaced 8,268 people across 13 departments. [URY-E-115]

## Differentiated vulnerability

- Source dated 2020-04-22 reports: 2011 census-block indicators include age under 15 or over 65, severe disability, crowding, insecure tenure, substandard housing, lack of piped water or sanitation, and informal settlements. [URY-E-102]
- Source dated 2020-04-22 reports: Heat vulnerability combines hazard with age, disability, water access and crowding; flood with crowding, sanitation, insecure tenure and informal settlements. [URY-E-103]
- Source dated 2020-04-22 reports: Juan Lacaze had no sewer network in study data; lower coverage counted as higher hydrometeorological exposure. [URY-E-104]
- The IMF reports a national agricultural-output decline of 25% year-on-year between late 2022 and mid-2023 during severe drought. Its soil-moisture modeling particularly covers Colonia, Durazno, Flores, Paysandú, Río Negro, San José and Soriano; the national loss is not assigned equally to those departments. [URY-E-107]
- The IMF's 2024 report describes rainfall/flood-related relocation and livelihood impacts for vulnerable groups during 2015–2019. The passage gives no locality, displacement duration or unique-person reconciliation; it does not establish violence or permanent migration. [URY-E-108]
- The IMF's modeled one-standard-deviation soil-moisture deficit shock produces a peak GDP contraction around 0.3% after three quarters, with primary-sector effects persisting for two years in SVAR and up to five in DSGE. This is a model experiment, not an observed or calendar-year forecast. Scenario: One-SD soil-moisture deficit in SVAR/DSGE; not an emissions scenario. [URY-E-109]
