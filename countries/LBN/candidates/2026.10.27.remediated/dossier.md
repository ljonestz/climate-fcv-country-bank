- Climate projections: The verified CCDR release provides major water and macroeconomic projections but not the underlying emissions scenario/model detail. More granular verified temperature, precipitation, wildfire, flood and sea-level projections are needed before populating a scenario-specific physical-risk layer. []
- Geographic/population differentiation: There is good urban/rural and some nationality/governorate differentiation, but much less traceable evidence for gender, disability, age, migrant workers, Palestine refugees, informal-settlement typologies and specific coastal/mountain livelihood groups. []
- Adaptation evaluation: The 2025 NAP, NDC 3.0 and current water/reconstruction operations are too recent - or insufficiently evaluated - to support strong effectiveness claims. Plans, financing and beneficiary targets remain intended capacity, not demonstrated outcomes. []
- Unintended effects: the non-climate-specific LHSP evaluation documents that geographically uneven infrastructure assistance can generate grievances, but this cannot establish a climate-adaptation pathway. Whether new resilience investments produce exclusion, elite capture, maladaptation or conflict-sensitive benefits remains an open question. []
- Causal uncertainty: The national climate-FCV evidence is dominated by perceptions and expert/programme synthesis. The reviewed sources do not identify an independent causal effect of temperature, rainfall or drought on violent events. []
- FCV-to-climate quantification: The qualitative/recovery evidence strongly supports reduced fiscal, infrastructure and institutional capacity after crisis/conflict, but few sources quantify how much this increases mortality, economic loss or service disruption during a given climate hazard. []
- Water-conflict generalization: The Bcharreh-Denniyeh/Qurnat al-Sawda case contains important governance lessons but cannot be generalized to all Lebanese water disputes; historical claims, property rights and local politics require separate tracing. []
- Food-security attribution: Recent acute food insecurity is documented, but the cited evidence identifies conflict, displacement and reduced assistance as principal immediate drivers. A climate share should not be inferred without agricultural/climate attribution evidence. []
- Current conflict/access conditions: displacement and service conditions are fast-changing. The June-July 2026 portal snapshot could not be recovered from the current rolling UNHCR page and was excluded; use dated operational products before screening a project location. []
- Source access limitation: The NDC 3.0 and NAP publication pages and download links were verified, but the detailed underlying PDFs were not independently parsed in this session. Detailed page-level policy claims therefore remain outside this packet. []

In particular, response-performance evidence is not climate-specific, and direct-climate-fcv evidence does not robustly establish causality. []
- UNDP's municipal survey represents 135 of 392 affected areas; 62 hard-to-reach areas were mostly southern border villages, and reported damage was preliminary and unvalidated. []
- The full FAO agriculture assessment could not be accessed through its official report endpoint (403) or a government-hosted mirror (502); claims are limited to the FAO launch summary. []

- The joint UN report's heat projection is regional and its emissions scenario is unspecified; do not treat 2024–2025 findings or policy recommendations as current 2026 outcomes. []
- The new reports assess 2023-2024 hostilities and 2024-2025 recovery, not current October 2026 damage or service conditions. []

## Climate pressures and exposure

- The World Bank projects climate change could reduce Lebanon's water availability by up to 9% by 2040 and up to 50% during the dry season. Scenario: 2040; emissions scenario not specified in accessible release [LBN-E-001]
- In Greater Beirut and Mount Lebanon, the World Bank states that private water tankers can cost up to ten times more than public supply alternatives addressed by the SGBWSP. [LBN-E-006]
- In Wave 22, 76.0% of respondents reported at least one listed climate-associated exposure; 66.3% reported water shortage and 59.2% excessive heat. [LBN-E-012]

## Differentiated vulnerability

- Climate impacts covered by the CCDR are projected to reduce Lebanon's growth potential by up to 2% annually by 2040. Scenario: 2040; CCDR modelling [LBN-E-002]
- The 2025 RDNA assessed US$6.8 billion in physical damage and US$7.2 billion in economic losses from the conflict over 8 Oct 2023-20 Dec 2024. [LBN-E-008]
- In the April-June 2025 IPC period cited by the FSAS guidance, about 1.17 million Lebanese residents, displaced Syrians and Palestine refugees were in IPC Phase 3+, with conflict, continued displacement and reduced humanitarian food assistance identified as main drivers. [LBN-E-011]
- Wave 22 respondents reporting climate-related household financial loss were more common in rural than urban areas (45.9% vs 37.3%) and among Syrian than Lebanese respondents (53.5% vs 37.6%). [LBN-E-013]
- FSAS guidance identifies political/sectarian tensions and threat of war as primary drivers of tensions among Lebanese, while competition over low-skilled jobs and service access is identified as a driver of Lebanese-Syrian tensions. [LBN-E-020]
- In the December 2024 municipal survey, 14,762 agricultural assets were reported damaged, with large counts in Baalbek/Bint Jbeil and high complete-damage shares in Nabatieh/Sour; environmental concerns were not each allocated geographically. [LBN-E-102]
- The FAO–Ministry–CNRS-L assessment attributes 2023–2024 agricultural impacts to conflict and estimates US$118 million in damage and US$586 million in losses, with Southern Lebanon and Bekaa most affected. [LBN-E-106]

## Coping and adaptive capacity

- The CCDR identifies Lebanon's limited adaptive capacity as having been further weakened by the economic and financial crisis, including through compromised public finances and deteriorating services. [LBN-E-003]
- 65.9% of Wave 22 respondents reported at least one household coping action; among copers, 60.1% cited using water wisely and 11.0% switching to renewable energy. [LBN-E-016]
- Although 32.6% reported using solar or other renewable power as supplemental electricity, the Wave 22 report interprets much of this uptake as necessity arising from Lebanon's electricity crisis rather than deliberate climate adaptation. [LBN-E-017]
- FAO's April 2025 launch summary estimates US$263 million in agricultural recovery needs, including US$95 million prioritized for 2025/26; proposed farm, irrigation and natural-resource restoration is not proven delivery. [LBN-E-107]

## Institutions and delivery systems

- Lebanon's 2025 NAP establishes a 2025-2035 adaptation roadmap covering eight climate-sensitive sectors and cross-cutting governance, finance, data/monitoring and stakeholder engagement. [LBN-E-004]
- Lebanon's NDC 3.0 explicitly identifies continuing gaps in data availability, institutional coordination and financial resources for climate implementation. [LBN-E-005]
- The World Bank reports that Lebanon's multiple crises have severely obstructed planning, implementation and maintenance of water infrastructure and hindered service sustainability. [LBN-E-007]
- LEAP is structured as a US$1 billion scalable recovery framework, with an initial US$250 million World Bank contribution to restore lifeline services and critical infrastructure. [LBN-E-009]
- The independent LHSP evaluation found that concentrating support in selected municipalities could leave neighbouring municipalities feeling excluded and create tensions; it documents one Akkar example and a Bekaa dispute over unequal solar-panel support. [LBN-E-021]
- The LHSP evaluation found no comprehensive programme-wide reporting and no analysis showing how outputs contributed to outcomes, limiting its ability to determine whether the programme reduced social tensions. [LBN-E-022]
- At the report date, UNDP described post-war recovery as constrained by institutional capacity weakened by prolonged fiscal and economic shocks, a political vacuum, and conditions restricting external aid. [LBN-E-101]

## Climate-FCV pathways

  - FCV mediator: Unequal access, affordability, service failure, perceived unfairness and pre-existing group/resource competition.
  - Possible consequence: Where governance and distribution are already contested, additional water stress could worsen grievances or intergroup tensions.

## Resilience and peace-supporting capacities

- Wave 22 found 78.8% perceived climate-related issues as increasing strain on basic services and 55.0% perceived them as contributing to tension/conflict. [LBN-E-014]
- The same survey found that attribution of climate pressures to tension was strongly shaped by pre-existing group relations and perceived competition over jobs/resources, with the climate-tension/negative Lebanese-Syrian relation association concentrated in Beirut and the Beqaa. [LBN-E-015]
- UNICEF reports that rehabilitation of a well in one divided North Lebanon community was accompanied by increased dialogue and reduced tension, informing later WASH/social-cohesion pilots. [LBN-E-018]
- ALEF describes the Bcharreh-Denniyeh dispute as involving contested land/water rights, groundwater recharge and pastoral livelihoods, episodic violence and weak governance; its evidence does not isolate climatic change as the cause. [LBN-E-019]
- Conflict-affected Bekaa and southern Lebanon are projected to face end-century wet-bulb globe temperature increases of 2–3.5°C; without adaptation, heat would reduce labour capacity, especially for outdoor manual workers. Scenario: Source-unspecified; the report gives an end-of-century projection but no emissions pathway. [LBN-E-103]
- The 2025 UN report describes attacks damaging at least 34 water facilities and affecting over 400,000 people amid pre-existing climate-related supply pressure; Rmeich disruption affected about 1,325 households, including 175 displaced families. [LBN-E-104]
- The report says military operations damaged natural habitats and coastal ecosystems, increasing Lebanon's vulnerability to climate change and biodiversity loss. [LBN-E-105]
