## 2. Climate baseline

Evidence: ERA5 reanalysis presents South Sudan's 1991-2020 climatology and 1950-2023 historical record at daily scale and 0.25-degree resolution; the portal describes a broad historical warming signal while cautioning that reanalysis is model-based and that precipitation trends are harder to distinguish from natural variability. [SSD-E-001]
Evidence: The 2025 SIPRI-NUPI fact sheet describes a tropical savannah climate with seasonal White Nile tributary flooding, severe drought and flood exposure, increasing rainy-season precipitation, declining dry-season rainfall, and consecutive floods that no longer reliably recede during the dry season. [SSD-E-002]
Evidence: The World Bank CCDR key messages project that hotter conditions could reduce sorghum yields by 2050. [SSD-E-003]

## 3. Vulnerability and capacity

Evidence: In flood-affected Rubkona, the SIPRI-NUPI synthesis reports declining livestock holdings and crop farming alongside greater reliance on aid, firewood, and wild foods; it also notes erosion of reciprocal household support and livelihood-related shifts in gender roles, indicating that repeated shocks and conflict can narrow customary coping options. [SSD-E-004]
Evidence: IOM's field validation in three flood-affected payams found damaged or dysfunctional public facilities and reduced road access to health care, markets, and schools; respondents also reported that access constraints prevented aid from reaching some populations and that much facility damage persisted for months. [SSD-E-005]
Evidence: The same IOM assessment found that floods made farming impossible in many assessed bomas, contributed to livestock loss, changed displacement duration, shifted some communities rapidly between hosting and displacement, and placed additional strain on resources available to communities receiving displaced people. [SSD-E-006]
Evidence: The UN Common Country Analysis reports that violence and very poor road infrastructure restrict humanitarian reach, rainy-season conditions make many roads impassable, and conflict and flooding together reduce access to drinking water, health, and nutrition services; it also identifies repeated new and secondary displacement. [SSD-E-007]
Evidence: The 2021 INFORM profile scores South Sudan high across hazard and exposure, vulnerability, and lack of coping capacity, with especially elevated components for uprooted people, governance, physical infrastructure, access to health care, food security, and violent conflict. [SSD-E-008]
Evidence: A UNFPA-led study summary from Malakal, Kapoeta South, and Rubkona reports that flood, drought, displacement, and economic stress coincide with restricted reproductive-health access, increased gender-based violence, child marriage, and survival-driven exploitation, while flooded facilities and heat events further strain health and education access. [SSD-E-009]
Evidence: IOM event tracking recorded 20,109 flood-related displacements during December 2024, concentrated in Twic, Rubkona, and Mayiendit counties, and displaced respondents identified health, shelter, and food as their most pressing needs. [SSD-E-010]
Evidence: South Sudan's first National Adaptation Plan states that priority sectors lacked concrete NDC implementation plans and identifies sector vulnerability assessment, capacity building, stronger policy and budget mainstreaming, early warning, and institutional coordination as measures needed to move national commitments toward implementation. [SSD-E-011]
Evidence: South Sudan's Second NDC sets adaptation priorities for climate-smart agriculture and livestock, water management, health-system resilience, flood and drought early warning, land-use planning, and inclusive participation by local communities, women, youth, civil society, and the private sector. [SSD-E-012]

## 4. Institutions, services, sectors, and livelihoods

Evidence: The SIPRI-NUPI fact sheet reports that 2024 flooding displaced people across 22 counties and that arrivals in high-ground areas of Unity State added population pressure where food insecurity, displacement, and limited coping capacity were already present; reports linked these pressures with local tensions. [SSD-E-013]
Evidence: Across the SIPRI-NUPI and UN climate-security syntheses, drought, flooding, and altered seasons are assessed as changing pasture, water, cropland, and pastoral movement conditions; where customary agreements and dispute-resolution mechanisms are weak, these shifts may heighten farmer-herder or cattle-group competition, while local dialogues have produced shared livelihood-protection rules. [SSD-E-014]
Evidence: SIPRI-NUPI documents cases where floodwater compounded landmine risk and prevented people from following evacuation advice, while prolonged inundation and violent land-grabbing constrained safe return; it also notes that some flood conditions produced temporary cooperation rather than violence. [SSD-E-015]
Evidence: The SIPRI-NUPI fact sheet and UN Common Country Analysis assess that conflict, political instability, weak rule of law and public institutions, limited infrastructure, and low trust reduce the capacity of households and institutions to prepare for, adapt to, and recover from climate-related shocks. [SSD-E-016]
Evidence: The Climate Security Mechanism brief identifies livelihood disruption, displacement and mobility, recruitment incentives, and elite exploitation of climate-related pressures as possible pathways linking climate stress and insecurity, and recommends conflict-sensitive peacebuilding, early warning, policy integration, and coordination as responses. [SSD-E-017]
Evidence: The Climate Security Mechanism reports that the 2024 conflict-sensitive flood response plan identified 243 high-ground locations and supported more than 30 awareness campaigns and dialogues, including a Yirol dialogue involving host communities, displaced people, and cattle keepers. [SSD-E-018]
Evidence: The Climate Security Mechanism describes localized forecasting, community knowledge, youth engagement, and dialogue as resilience capacities, while identifying warning communication, language, financing, and supply gaps that can limit timely preparedness in fragile settings. [SSD-E-019]

## 8. Uncertainties

Uncertainty: ERA5 is model-based; national aggregation masks local variation and supports no social attribution. [SSD-E-001]
Uncertainty: Sparse gauges and synthesized datasets limit subnational trend and site-level inference. [SSD-E-002]
Uncertainty: Only official CCDR key messages were accessible; project exposure still needs local assessment. [SSD-E-003]
Uncertainty: Selected qualitative communities are not nationally representative; conflict, markets, and aid also shape coping. [SSD-E-004]
Uncertainty: Three purposively assessed payams are not nationally representative despite substantial participation. [SSD-E-005]
Uncertainty: Purposive local coverage documents co-occurrence, not a single mechanism producing tension. [SSD-E-006]
Uncertainty: Mixed reference periods support compound-risk screening, not separate conflict and flood effects. [SSD-E-007]
Uncertainty: The national composite masks subnational variation and supports screening, not causal inference. [SSD-E-008]
Uncertainty: A summary of three high-exposure locations cannot establish national prevalence. [SSD-E-009]
Uncertainty: One month of reported movements is neither a displacement stock nor long-term assessment. [SSD-E-010]
Uncertainty: The 2021-2025 NAP diagnoses gaps and plans reforms; subsequent implementation requires verification. [SSD-E-011]
Uncertainty: NDC commitments and intended measures do not demonstrate consistent implementation or achieved resilience. [SSD-E-012]
Uncertainty: Local associations do not establish a deterministic pathway; governance, assistance, and prior disputes matter. [SSD-E-013]
Uncertainty: Climate and mobility are rarely sole drivers; weapons, elite incentives, disease, and disputes matter. [SSD-E-014]
Uncertainty: Documented examples are not nationally prevalent; mines, armed actors, land claims, and cooperation alter outcomes. [SSD-E-015]
Uncertainty: The synthesis cannot isolate institutional or conflict effects from poverty, displacement, and infrastructure deficits. [SSD-E-016]
Uncertainty: Possible pathways are not quantitative attribution and remain conditional on local conflict and political economy. [SSD-E-017]
Uncertainty: Reported activities lack independent outcome evaluation and evidence of consistent implementation. [SSD-E-018]
Uncertainty: Program experience does not compare the effectiveness of different resilience or conflict interventions. [SSD-E-019]

## 9. Project-screening implications

Screening implication: Use ERA5 as light climate context, retaining its model and precipitation-trend caveats. [SSD-E-001]
Screening implication: Screen for changing flood persistence and dry-season water stress, not a single uniform hazard trend. [SSD-E-002]
Screening implication: Screen agrifood investments for projected heat-related sorghum yield risk through 2050. [SSD-E-003]
Screening implication: Assess whether assistance strengthens viable livelihoods and reciprocal coping rather than replacing them. [SSD-E-004]
Screening implication: Map all-season access to facilities and aid routes, including the duration of flood disruption. [SSD-E-005]
Screening implication: Screen livelihood recovery and host-community capacity together because flood displacement can reverse hosting roles. [SSD-E-006]
Screening implication: Treat violence, seasonal roads, service functionality, and secondary displacement as one access-risk screen. [SSD-E-007]
Screening implication: Use INFORM to flag compound vulnerability and coping constraints, not to rank individual project sites. [SSD-E-008]
Screening implication: Integrate GBV, child-marriage, reproductive-health, and girls' education safeguards into climate response. [SSD-E-009]
Screening implication: Use current event tracking to size immediate health, shelter, and food needs without treating it as a displacement stock. [SSD-E-010]
Screening implication: Check whether projects close documented planning, coordination, capacity, budget, and early-warning gaps. [SSD-E-011]
Screening implication: Align activities with NDC sector priorities while testing participation and implementation capacity locally. [SSD-E-012]
Screening implication: Plan high-ground relocation with host-resource, land-use, food-security, and coexistence safeguards. [SSD-E-013]
Screening implication: Support negotiated mobility and resource-sharing rules before changing water, pasture, or cropland access. [SSD-E-014]
Screening implication: Assess mines, contested tenure, land-grabbing, and negotiated access before evacuation, return, or relocation. [SSD-E-015]
Screening implication: Screen whether conflict and institutional weakness are constraining climate adaptation capacity. [SSD-E-016]
Screening implication: Climate-security pathways can pass through livelihoods, mobility, recruitment, and elite exploitation [SSD-E-017]
Screening implication: Conflict-sensitive flood response combined high-ground preparation with community dialogue [SSD-E-018]

Screening implication: Repeated flooding may increase localized host-displaced tension when displacement concentrates people on scarce high ground with limited services and livelihood resources. [SSD-P-001; SSD-E-002, SSD-E-006, SSD-E-010, SSD-E-013, SSD-E-018]

Screening implication: Flooded infrastructure and insecurity can jointly restrict service and humanitarian access, with possible implications for exclusion and grievance. [SSD-P-003; SSD-E-005, SSD-E-007]

Screening implication: Conflict legacies and contested land can restrict climate-related evacuation, return, or relocation where routes and destinations are unsafe or disputed. [SSD-P-007; SSD-E-015]
