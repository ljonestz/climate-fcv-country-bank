## Climate pressures and exposure

- The CCDR estimates about 463 km2 of urban area is at high risk of flooding from rivers, rain and sea-level rise; many affected urban residents live in unplanned settlements without adequate drainage. [TZA-E-001]
- The CCDR estimates 27.7 percent of Tanzania's population and 29.4 percent of its vulnerable population are exposed to at least one climate risk. [TZA-E-002]
- The NDC reports average maximum temperatures rose over 1 degree C in 1961-2013 and drying occurred in northeastern and much of southern Tanzania during 1981-2016, affecting agriculture, water and energy. [TZA-E-004]
- NDC projects 0.8-1.8 degrees C warming by the 2040s and 1.6-5.0 degrees C by 2090; dry-season rainfall may decline, wet-season rainfall rise, with southern decline and Lake Victoria-area increase by 2030. Scenario: The inspected summary does not specify it. [TZA-E-005]
- Source dated 2025-03 reports: The audit's cited July 2024 report says the 2023 Hanang mudslide affected 261 houses, destroyed 95, damaged 754.875 acres of farmland and disrupted 742 businesses. [TZA-E-101]
- Source dated 2025-03 reports: In Kilosa, auditors observed a Standard Gauge Railway stormwater culvert without a continuous downstream channel; accumulated floodwater affected neighboring areas. [TZA-E-102]
- The ministry's 2024 sector report describes above-average Rufiji rainfall and high Great Ruaha flow at Msembe during the 2023–2024 season. [TZA-E-104]

## Differentiated vulnerability

- The CCDR says about 70 percent of Tanzania's population depends on agriculture, many as poor smallholder farmers, and is poorly positioned to manage repeated or simultaneous climate shocks. [TZA-E-003]
- Source dated not stated reports: The Dodoma project describes its Chamwino and Mpwapwa target communities as groundwater-dependent; unreliable rainfall affects crops and livestock and is associated with drought, flooding, erosion and limited recharge. [TZA-E-111]

## Coping and adaptive capacity

- The NDC sets a target to raise access to clean, safe water from 2015 baselines of 86 percent in urban areas and 67.7 percent in rural areas to 100 percent by 2030. [TZA-E-006]
- Source dated not stated reports: The completed project reports community-led plans adopted by Chamwino and Mpwapwa councils, 25 adaptation actions added to 2024/25 plans and budgets, and TZS 500 million approved for implementation. [TZA-E-112]

## Institutions and delivery systems

- Source dated 2025-03 reports: In 2024, none of five visited councils had demarcated or protected identified flood-prone areas; the audit tables show no disaster-coordination allocations in Kinondoni, Hanang, Mbeya or Kilosa, unlike Rufiji. [TZA-E-103]
- Source dated 2025-03 reports: The Ministry assigns local water allocation to Water User Associations and rural-scheme operation to community water organizations; it reports staff, data, storage and financing gaps in 2024. [TZA-E-105]
- Source dated 2025 reports: A 2025 mixed-methods study across Monduli and Longido reports that responsiveness, accountability, transparency and stakeholder participation significantly influenced local adaptation implementation, with participation strongest. [TZA-E-107]
- Source dated 2026-08-24 reports: The brief cites estimates that preparedness receives about 25% of the disaster-management budget, while the Water Ministry assigns about 6% to water-resource management versus 75% to water supply. [TZA-E-108]
- Source dated 2026-08-24 reports: The 43-interview study identifies reactive-response norms, budget ceilings, institutional silos, weak coordination authority and generic monitoring as mutually reinforcing reasons flood and drought preparedness is underfunded. [TZA-E-109]
- Source dated 2026-08-24 reports: The brief recommends preparedness rules in budget guidelines, costed and coordinated ministry plans, and transparent tracking of planned and actual preparedness spending. [TZA-E-110]
